1. What do photojournalists do?

A photojournalist goes to where the news happens and takes pictures. There are strict rules:

  • Show what really happened.
  • Never stage a photo. Don't ask people to pose or move things.
  • Never change the photo with editing.
  • Write a true caption: who, what, where, when.

2. Why are photos evidence?

A photo records a place, a time and people. It can show things a police report leaves out. But a photo also has limits. Often the photographer arrives after something happens.

3. One photo, July 2016

The story of a photo
Pasay City, Manila · July 2016

Late at night, men on a motorcycle shot Michael Siaron, a 30-year-old pedicab driver, while he waited for passengers. Someone left a piece of cardboard at the scene with one word on it: "pusher."

Raffy Lerma, a photographer for the Philippine Daily Inquirer, took a photo of Michael's partner, Jennelyn Olaires, holding him in the street. The newspaper put it on the front page. Many people compared it to famous religious paintings of a mother holding her son.

Some people online said the photo looked staged. Photographers who were there answered: the light came from police floodlights and TV cameras, and nobody set anything up.

On 25 July 2016, in a speech to the nation, President Duterte talked about the photo. He called it drama.

We do not show the photo here because it shows a person who has died.

4. Why does a photographer do this work?

In his own words
Daniel Berehulak, photojournalist · won the 2017 Pulitzer Prize for his drug war photos

In 2016, Daniel Berehulak spent more than a month in Manila taking photos of the drug war for The New York Times. In interviews, he explained why he does this work:

  • A journalist first. He says a photojournalist must be a better journalist than photographer: check the facts, and explain what is happening.
  • People, not "subjects." He doesn't like to call the people in his photos "subjects." They have names, and they share their stories with him.
  • The truth of the moment. He will not send a photo that gives a wrong idea of what happened.
  • Who died. He saw that most of the dead were poor. He called it "a war on class."

We do not link to his photos because they show people who have died.

  1. Berehulak says the people in his photos have names. Why is that important? Think about Kian in Stage 2.
  2. He called it "a war on class." What does he mean? Which source from Stage 2 agrees with him?
  3. Is taking these photos a way of being an upstander? Why or why not?

Two kinds of upstanders

Reading 1

The nightcrawlers

Journalists and photographers in Manila · 2016–2017

During the drug war, a small group of journalists in Manila worked through the night. People called them the nightcrawlers. When someone was killed, they went to the place quickly, before the evidence was gone. They wrote down names and took photographs. They believed that no one should be killed without due process.

Their work had a cost. Vincent Go, a photographer, said he saw the faces of the victims in his sleep. Some journalists were attacked online. When newspapers would not print his photos, Ezra Acayan shared them on social media.

In December 2016, Brother Jun Santiago, a religious brother at Baclaran Church in Manila, began going out at night with them. He said, "People are sleeping." He wanted people to wake up and see. He and Vincent Go made an exhibit of photos in the church. More than 100,000 people saw it, and 13 other churches asked for copies. Some families said the photos showed that their loved ones mattered.

Other photographers, like Raffy Lerma and Daniel Berehulak, also recorded the killings. In 2017, Berehulak won the Pulitzer Prize for Breaking News Photography. The prize judges said his photos showed "callous disregard for human life" in the Philippines.

Reading 2

Families go to court

Residents of San Andres Bukid, Manila · October 2017

San Andres Bukid is a poor area of Manila. It has 26 small neighborhoods, called barangays. Many people there were killed in police operations.

On 18 October 2017, 39 residents from all 26 barangays asked the Supreme Court for help. Their lawyers came from a group called the Center for International Law. The residents said they did not feel safe from the police in their area.

They asked for a writ of amparo. This is a special court order. It protects a person whose life or safety is in danger. They asked the court to keep the police away from their families, to move the police officers to another area, and to investigate 35 deaths.

Going to court was brave. The people they were afraid of were the police. Earlier that year, in a similar case from another area, the Supreme Court had ordered the police to stay one kilometer away from a group of families.

What did the court decide for San Andres Bukid? We could not find a clear answer. Some news reports say the case was still waiting for a decision years later.
